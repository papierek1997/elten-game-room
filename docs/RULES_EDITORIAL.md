# Writing game rules for players

A rulebook should sound like a clear explanation from someone who knows the
game. A newcomer needs to understand the aim, what happens during play,
the consequences of their choices and how to carry out those choices in
Power Games. They should not have to reconstruct the game from a list of
features, settings or fixes, or keep consulting a shortcut reference to
find out how to perform the action just described.

Natural language is the main requirement, not a finishing touch after filling
in a template. Use the structure below to organise the explanation, but let
the game determine the headings, paragraph lengths and examples. Do not make
every rulebook sound like another copy of the same document.
This does not require original wording for familiar subjects: headings such
as “Scoring” or “How to play” can recur wherever they fit.

The reader has opened the game for the first time and wants to start playing.
Optimise for the time it takes them to understand and act, not the number of
words they have to read. A slightly longer explanation can be quicker to use
than a short formula that requires rereading or asking another player.

Read this guide in full before writing, importing, revising or translating
game rules, including rules supplied in a PR. It applies in every language.
It follows the approach to natural wording and separate editorial review in
the [quiz guidelines](QUIZ_EDITORIAL.md), adapted to a continuous explanation.
It does not authorise rewriting unrelated rulebooks or overriding a ready-to-use
text supplied or selected by its creator. The distinction below takes precedence
over stylistic advice throughout this guide.

## Respect rules supplied by their creator

All newly written rules must follow this guide, including rules written by AI
as part of creating a game. A request such as "create a game" includes writing
its rules to this standard unless the creator supplies or selects existing
rules to use. This applies regardless of who is credited as the game's or
rulebook's author. A byline or approval of a working draft does not, by itself,
exempt newly drafted rules from editorial work.

When a creator directly supplies rules for the game or points to existing
rules as the text to use, preserve that wording. This includes
text pasted into a message, supplied in a file or PR, or identified in the
project, whether before creating the game or as a later replacement. The
creator need not know this guide or add a special "do not rewrite" instruction.
AI must not rewrite that text, append explanations or replace it with a
template-based version without explicit permission. A different structure,
tone or way of explaining the game is not permission to edit it. Neither is a
general instruction to follow this guide or keep documentation up to date.

Material explicitly provided only as a source or inspiration is not a
ready-to-use rulebook: use it to write new rules under this guide. If its
intended role is unclear, ask whether to use the supplied wording or write
a new explanation before substantially rewriting it. Do not guess from the
style whether a human or AI wrote it; either can produce a text the creator
has chosen to keep. An explicit request to edit or rewrite authorises that
work within the requested scope, without another confirmation.

If a protected text appears outdated, incomplete or inconsistent with the game,
report the specific passage and the verified discrepancy separately. Do not
silently fix it, insert a warning into the rulebook or invent a missing rule.
Ask the creator or maintainer to decide whether to change the text. Permission
to correct one passage does not extend to the rest of the book; permission
to format a document does not include permission to rewrite its wording.

For a supplied or selected text, an explicit translation request permits a
faithful translation, not a new rulebook in the author's name. Preserve its
meaning, scope, voice, examples and credits; do not add your own explanations
or remove material merely because it differs from this template. Existing
translations that the creator supplies or selects as the text to use have
the same protection. Keep proposed corrections separate until they are
approved. Apply the ordinary
editorial guidance below to newly commissioned rules and to revisions whose
scope has actually been authorised.

## Start with the game

Work out what a beginner needs to know to play a first turn or rally. Read the
agreed sources and check how the Power Games version differs. Use the code to
confirm those differences, legal moves, scoring and available variants, not
as an outline for the prose. A method, setting or release-note entry does not
automatically deserve a paragraph in the rules.

Before drafting, make a short working outline of the objective, starting
situation, normal play, important exceptions and ending. Keep sources and
unresolved questions in editorial notes. Do not guess a rule or describe a
suspected bug as intentional behaviour; resolve it before presenting the
explanation as finished. Preserve required attribution and licences without
copying a source's wording wholesale.

Teach the complete default game first. Put optional changes in a separate
variants part after normal play and scoring; do not announce them in the
opening or interrupt an ordinary action with an alternative setting. This
includes optional scoring, different drawing rules and changed winning
conditions. Explain each variant as a change to the game already taught.

Distinguish a prerequisite in the default rules from an optional change.
For example, needing a first meld before taking a discard belongs beside
drawing. Ending a turn differently when discarding is disabled belongs in
that variant, not in the first explanation of a turn. Do not reassure the
reader that an ordinary action also works with an as-yet-unexplained option
turned off: that introduces the option early and suggests a dependency
where none exists.

Select information before polishing its wording. Keep a detail when it
helps the player understand a rule, make a choice, recognise a game cue or
perform an action. Remove details that do none of these, rather than merely
shortening their sentences. Do not document every observable behaviour of
the program. For example, a spectator's perspective does not need a paragraph
explaining that it persists between points, changes the order of the spoken
score and does not let the spectator move a paddle. The perspective keys
can remain in the shortcut reference.

Describe what the player can do or learn in this version of the game, not
an imagined physical tabletop. A face-down or face-up pile does not need
a setup description when the player only draws a card or reads the last
discard. Keep spatial descriptions where they guide actual play, such as
board coordinates or the audible position of a ball. Do not define familiar
words or add a negative example solely to guard against every imaginable
misreading. Once a set requires different suits, another sentence ruling
out a second queen of hearts adds no new rule.

There are two different editorial decisions here. Remove a topic when it
does not help someone play. When the topic is needed, explain it fully enough
to be understood. Do not meet a length target by dropping the verbs,
connections or intermediate actions from an essential explanation. This is
not a reason to cut a meaningful exception or the instructions a beginner
actually needs.

## Teach before providing a reminder

An explanation for a newcomer and a reference for someone who already knows
the game have different jobs. [Resonym's discussion of rulebook writing](https://resonym.com/writing-rulebooks/)
describes this conflict and the value of testing whether people can learn
from the text without its author explaining it to them.

In Power Games, use the main chapters to teach and the controls section to
help players recall keys. Clear headings also let returning players find a
rule. This does not require two separate rulebooks or a second copy of all
the rules.

Give the reader a short view of an ordinary turn, round or rally before
explaining its individual decisions. Then follow those decisions in a useful
order. Do not interrupt the first turn with every card effect, unusual
combination or optional setting. Put substantial detail in a clearly named
subsection, while keeping an immediate restriction beside the action it
affects. Do not promise an action unconditionally and reveal much later that
it is normally forbidden.

The first explanation should answer the beginner's next real question. If
they have just learned that a red light stops their car, explain how to get
moving again. If they have just placed a letter, explain whether that submits
their word or whether another action is needed. This is a way to order the
explanation, not a compulsory question-and-answer format.

Do not turn this approach into a fixed script with the same opening and
three-sentence rhythm for every game. A continuous exchange in Pong, a
card-playing turn and a word-building turn need different explanations.

## Write headings that name the subject

A heading should tell readers what they will find, including when they
hear it on its own in the contents. Use ordinary, descriptive wording.
Do not make titles more original by turning them into slogans, metaphors,
scene descriptions or hints whose meaning becomes clear only afterwards.
Natural language does not require novelty. “Punktacja”, “Przebieg tury”,
“Gra w drużynach” and “Ustawienia dźwięku” are useful Polish headings when
they match the content. It is fine for several games to use them.

Examples from the drafts show the distinction:

- “Rakietka między Tobą a bramką” describes an unclear spatial relationship,
  not a subject. For an introduction explaining the aim and basic play,
  use “Na czym polega gra”. A section actually teaching movement could be
  “Sterowanie rakietką”; do not use that title for a section without controls.
- “Z rzutu do karty punktacji” compresses a process into an awkward phrase.
  “Rzuty kośćmi i zapisywanie punktów” names the two actions being taught.
- “Jedna tura, jeden limit czasu” sounds like a slogan. “Czas na ruch”
  identifies the rule a player is looking for.

These are not a phrase blacklist. Read all headings without the body and
check whether a beginner can predict their content and find a particular
rule. Then check each heading against its actual section. If it takes a
long list of subjects to name a section, reconsider how that section is
organised. Do not replace an unclear title with an equally vague “Basics”.

In every language, choose the normal wording for that kind of instruction.
Translate the heading's purpose, not an awkward image from another language.
A grammatical phrase may still be something no one would naturally use as
a rulebook heading. Plain subject labels and useful, natural questions are
both acceptable; there is no required formula or word count.

## Write connected, natural prose

Prefer familiar words, concrete actions and clear subjects. Address the player
consistently, using the conventions of the target language. Natural does not
mean chatty, imprecise or deliberately informal; forced jokes and decorative
introductions do not make an instruction more human.

Choose simple, commonly used words over unusual, literary or official-sounding
alternatives when they mean the same thing. An easy, conversational tone is
welcome, but do not force slang or expressions that may be unfamiliar to
players from another region or generation. Explain a necessary game term
rather than replacing it with a rare synonym.

### Sentence rhythm and the amount of information

Neither a chain of short commands nor a long sentence packed with conditions
makes a good explanation. Join closely related ideas when the connection
helps the reader, and start a new sentence when the thought changes. A short
sentence can emphasise an important limit; a longer one can explain a choice
and its consequence. Do not alternate lengths mechanically or impose a
word-count rule.

Give a paragraph one main purpose. Explain that idea, then add a condition or
example if needed. Avoid introducing several new terms, a scoring exception,
a variant and a shortcut in the same paragraph. When there is too much to
absorb, change the order or split the topic; merely cutting a long sentence
into five short ones does not solve the problem.

Short sentences can still be dense. A string of names and effects asks the
reader to reconstruct the actions and their order. Check what is missing,
not only how long the sentence is. Name the player, card or action again
when a pronoun or omitted verb would make the meaning uncertain. Use words
such as “first”, “after” or “instead” when that relationship is part of the
rule; do not invent a sequence just to make the paragraph flow.

Read whole paragraphs and neighbouring sections, not just individual
sentences. Repeated openings, lists disguised as paragraphs and abrupt jumps
between subjects often become apparent only during a continuous read-through.

### Check what the words actually mean

Grammatically correct wording can still name the wrong thing or obscure the
rule. Cards are shuffled, not earlier plays: in Polish, use “wcześniej zagrane
karty”, not “wcześniejsze zagrania”. Say when someone is eliminated and who
wins, rather than describing “the last person who does not drop out for
points”. Read the action and its subject literally before accepting a phrase.

Explain the effect that matters to the player. A distance card “brings you
closer to the finish”, rather than “adding distance to your route”. Check
that the chosen verb describes what really changes; a fluent metaphor must
not suggest a different rule. Once the context is clear, omit unnecessary
qualifiers: “o daną liczbę mil” is enough without “o podaną na niej liczbę
mil”. Keep qualifiers that actually restrict when or how an action is allowed.

For a rule about crossing a scoring threshold, a small numerical example can
be clearer than a formula written out in words. Keep the distinction between
reaching the exact value and passing it; do not simplify away a real rule.
Explain cooperation in a team before giving seat letters or shared counters.
Describe an audio cue in terms the player can recognise, and direct them to
the tutorial when the sound must be learned. Do not invent a sound description
that may be false for another available sound pack.

Distinguish a suggestion from a requirement. Do not introduce a legal action
with an invented condition such as “If none of your cards is useful, discard
one” when the player may discard instead of playing any card. Say what the
choice does and when it is available. Replace vague phrases such as “improve
your situation” or “check your progress” with the actual action or information,
or remove the sentence if it adds nothing. Explain a shortcut after teaching
the information it reads; a list of unexplained terms is not an introduction.

Do not add a negative restatement after a complete positive rule unless it
explains a real exception or a likely mistake. Sometimes the right edit is
to delete the sentence. A warning that an ordinary matching card does not
cancel a drawing penalty adds little when the preceding explanation already
states which cards can answer that penalty.

### Use lists for enumerations

When enumerating several distinct things, make them a list rather than
packing them into a paragraph. This applies throughout the rules, not only
to shortcuts or optional settings: types of melds, movements of pieces,
card effects, scoring values, ways a round can end and comparable choices
all benefit from separate entries. A list lets the reader pause or return
to one item without searching through unrelated sentences.

Introduce the shared idea, then give each item its own explanation. Keep
the verbs, conditions and useful examples; a bullet does not justify
shorthand that a beginner has to decode. For example:

> Na stół wykładasz układy złożone z co najmniej trzech kart. Są dwa rodzaje
> układów:
>
> - Seria to kolejne karty w jednym kolorze, na przykład 5, 6 i 7 kier.
>   Możesz ją przedłużyć czwórką lub ósemką kier.
> - Zestaw tworzą trzy albo cztery karty tej samej wartości w różnych
>   kolorach, na przykład dama kier, dama karo i dama pik. Do tego zestawu
>   pasuje jeszcze dama trefl.

Use unordered Markdown bullets for an enumeration. Reserve numbered lists
for a genuine sequence of steps; do not number independent alternatives
as if they had to be performed in order. Keep lists shallow and separate
them from surrounding text with a blank line. Do not break ordinary connected
prose into bullets just because each sentence contains another fact. A
complete turn can still be taught in paragraphs, while the different card
types encountered during that turn belong in a list.

In rulebook paragraph data, put each bullet in its own paragraph starting
with `- `. The rulebook view recognises these as native list items, preserving
their text, chapter navigation and screen-reader list navigation. Do not
add raw Markdown headings or emphasis to these paragraphs: chapters already
have their own native headings. In Markdown documents, use standard Markdown
list syntax and normal heading structure.

The separate keyboard-shortcut reference already displays each entry as a
native list row. Keep one shortcut per paragraph there, without adding a
Markdown bullet. This does not replace explaining the relevant keys beside
the actions in the main rules.

### Worked editorial examples

These are focused excerpts showing the required style, not complete rules
or text to paste into every game. Check the relevant variant and current
controls before adapting them. The card-playing controls in the first
example belong in the preceding explanation of a turn; they do not need to
be repeated for every card.

#### Explain the sequence instead of listing effects

This passage from the rejected Mille Bornes draft is short but hard to learn
from:

> Żeby ruszyć, zagraj zielone światło. Dopiero wtedy możesz wykładać dystans.
> Czerwone światło zatrzymuje przeciwnika i wymaga od niego kolejnego zielonego.
> Brak paliwa usuwa karta paliwa, przebicie opony koło zapasowe, a wypadek naprawa.
> Po usunięciu tych trzech usterek potrzeba jeszcze zielonego światła.

“Wykładać dystans” hides the card action behind an unnatural phrase. The
repair sentence is a compressed matching list, while “tych trzech usterek”
can suggest that all three problems have to be fixed together. Replacing
commas with full stops would leave the underlying explanation unchanged.

A clearer version teaches driving, stopping and recovering in order:

> Żeby rozpocząć jazdę, zagraj kartę zielonego światła. Możesz wtedy zagrywać
> karty dystansu i pokonywać kolejne mile. Przeciwnik może cię zatrzymać
> czerwonym światłem — do dalszej jazdy będziesz potrzebować kolejnego zielonego.
>
> Nie każdą przeszkodę usuniesz jednak zielonym światłem. Jeśli przeciwnik
> przebije ci oponę, najpierw zagraj koło zapasowe. Po wypadku potrzebujesz
> karty naprawy, a gdy zabraknie paliwa — karty paliwa. Po usunięciu przeszkody
> zagraj zielone światło, żeby znów ruszyć.

The extra words connect the actions; they do not add interface trivia or
repeat an unrelated rule. The important distinction is between removing a
problem and being able to drive again. In the full rulebook, explain the
safety cards that change this sequence in their own relevant section.

Do not turn this into a ban on compact lists. Once the mechanism is clear,
a short card-effect reference may be useful. It cannot replace the first
explanation.

#### Explain how to carry out a decision

“Wybierz pole, połóż literę i zatwierdź słowo” names the intended actions but
does not teach a first-time Scrabble player how to perform them. A usable
excerpt is:

> Strzałkami przejdź na pole, na którym chcesz położyć literę, i naciśnij Enter.
> Z listy wybierz literę ze swojego stojaka i zatwierdź ją Enterem. W ten sam
> sposób połóż pozostałe litery słowa.
>
> Przed zatwierdzeniem możesz jeszcze poprawić ułożenie liter. Żeby zdjąć
> dołożoną literę, przejdź na jej pole i naciśnij Backspace. Gotowe słowo
> zatwierdź klawiszem F.

Choosing a tile and submitting a word are separate decisions. The controls
make that distinction usable without documenting the entire interface.
The preceding rules must still explain where a word may legally be placed.

#### Remove irrelevant detail rather than compressing it

An explanation of spectator perspective does not need a compact replacement
for every detail about score order, persistence between points and inability
to control another paddle. If those details do not help someone play, remove
them. Keep the useful perspective keys in the reference. By contrast, a
requirement to play green after repairing a car changes what a player may do
and must remain explained.

This is the distinction to preserve throughout a rewrite: remove an
unnecessary topic, but keep the connections inside a necessary explanation.

### Words, terms and examples

Use an ordinary verb when it says the same thing: “dobierasz kartę” rather
than “dokonujesz operacji dobrania karty”, or “choose a card” rather than
“perform the selection of a card”. Avoid automatic introductions such as
“it is important to note” and repeated assurances about what the program
handles correctly. Keep an introduction only when it adds useful context.

Do not replace precise game terms with obscure synonyms merely to sound
different. First explain an unfamiliar idea through what players do, then
name it if the term will be useful later. In Polish, replacing “lewa” with
“wziątka” does not help a beginner. A short introductory description can
avoid both, while the full rules can explain how cards are collected before
using the established term. Keep that term consistent afterwards.

Choose examples that resolve a real difficulty: following suit, using a
joker, counting a bonus or deciding whether a round has ended. Use only the
pieces, numbers and conditions needed for the example. Check it against the
actual variant; a fluent but illegal example teaches the wrong game. Do not
add an example to every paragraph just to follow a pattern. State the rule
outside the example too: an example may demonstrate rounding down, but must
not be the only place where the reader can discover that scores round down.
Do not hide another restriction or exception inside an illustration.

Put essential information where a player would look for it. A safety-card
list should name every hazard that card protects against, including an
optional hazard explained later. A brief mention there does not mean teaching
the whole variant early: “Driving Ace protects against accidents and driving
against traffic” is enough; explain the extra hazard in its own variant.
Do not make the reader search several chapters to discover that a summary
left out a relevant effect.

Be concise without removing the explanation. “Draw and end your turn” is not
an adequate summary if the drawn card can be played immediately. Preserve
the difference between must, may and can only, as well as turn order,
prerequisites, exceptions and the distinction between a round and a match.
Do not turn a conditional rule into an absolute one for the sake of brevity.

## An adaptable outline

The outline is a drafting aid, not a set of compulsory chapter titles. Combine
short sections and add a chapter when a substantial mechanic needs one.
Explain continuous play as a rally or exchange rather than inventing turns
for an audio game. A quiz may need a section on answering rather than moves.

### What you are trying to achieve

Open with the idea of the game and how you win. Give enough context to make
the first action meaningful, without starting with every scoring exception.
Mention the number of players where it helps explain play or teams. Avoid
promotional claims and a catalogue of supported features.

### How play begins

Explain the starting position, what each player receives and who begins, as
far as these affect the game. Include a genuine prerequisite, such as voice
communication for Taboo. Explain any game-specific preparation and the
controls needed to do it, such as placing ships. Do not repeat the shared
table-creation procedure or count every press of Tab.

Distinguish preparation the player performs from a position the game has
already set up. Do not make the introduction a manual setup inventory when
no such action is needed. Still explain how to understand the board: blind
players may need its layout and coordinates. Separate that orientation from
a detailed starting-position reference instead of packing the board size,
piece counts, new terminology and every starting square into one paragraph.

### What happens during play

Follow the order in which the player encounters decisions. Explain what they
may do, when a move is legal, what it changes and when play passes on. Place
important exceptions beside the rule they qualify. Teach a complicated
mechanic in stages rather than mentioning it before its prerequisites.

When introducing a player action, include the key, button or short menu
path used to perform it. This applies to ordinary actions too, not only
unusual ones. Explain choosing and confirming separately when the game
requires both. For instance, a Scrabble explanation needs to distinguish
placing a tile from submitting the word. Give enough context to find the
control, but normally teach one straightforward route rather than every
alternative. Do not repeat the same key each time the action is mentioned.

In a Polish Pong explanation, “Rakietką poruszasz strzałkami w lewo i w prawo”
teaches the action and its control together. When discussing its personal
settings, include “Ustawienia otworzysz przy stole skrótem Ctrl+P”. Saying
only that the paddle moves or that settings are available leaves the reader
without a way to use them. Verify these controls in the current interface;
examples in this guide do not establish bindings for another game.

For an audio game, explain the sounds that let the player locate and react
to the action. For a board game, explain its layout without relying on a
picture or on phrases such as “as shown above”. Essential ways of perceiving
the game belong here; implementation details about playing audio do not.

### Scoring and the end of play

Explain when points are awarded or lost and who wins. Where relevant,
distinguish ending a turn, ending a round, elimination and winning the whole
match. Include a blocked game or a tie where it changes the outcome. Use a
small worked example when calculation is otherwise hard to follow.

### Variants

Describe what each variant changes relative to the game just explained.
Keep this material together, after the complete default game. Do not preview
an elimination mode in the opening, insert optional identical-card melds
among the basic melds, or add a variant's turn-ending shortcut to the normal
turn. The final shortcut reference may include all variants once taught.
State the default when it matters, and identify incompatible options where
players need to choose between them. Group related choices; do not paste
every configuration-field label and its technical range into the rules.
An option that changes a legal move, penalty or winning condition must still
be explained, even if it is not enabled by default. Identify where choices
are made: table settings before play or personal settings opened at the
table. When describing personal settings, give their actual shortcut or
menu path beside the explanation, not only in the reference at the end.

### Controls

The dedicated shortcuts section is a quick reference, not the only place
that explains how to play. Keep it consistent with the actual F1 help and
the instructions in the main text. Give each shortcut its own short action
description, for example “D: odczytaj wynik rzutu”. Do not compress unrelated
keys into one sentence or add “Naciśnij …, aby …” to every entry.

The main text teaches an action in context; this list helps a player recall
its key later. That limited repetition is useful and should not be removed
as duplication. Avoid repeating the whole explanation in the list. Shared
chat, invitations, table management and window navigation still belong in
the user guide and contextual help.

## Keep each kind of information in its place

Use this distinction when revising a rulebook that has grown through updates:

| Information | Where it belongs |
| --- | --- |
| Legal moves, scoring, penalties, winning, meaningful variants | Game rules |
| How to recognise the ball's position or another essential game cue | Relevant explanation in the rules, with an audio tutorial link if available |
| How to perform an action being taught, or open game-specific settings | Beside its explanation in the rules, using the actual key, button or short path |
| Complete game-specific shortcut reference | Dedicated controls section, as a concise reminder |
| Chat, invitations, switching windows, general sound and speech settings | Player README or shared help |
| What was fixed or added in a release | Changelog |
| Synchronisation, callbacks, storage, protocol details, compatibility workarounds | Technical documentation |
| Authorship, adaptation credits and required source notices | A clearly identified credits/source section and the required notice files |

For example, Axel Pong's rules need to explain moving the paddle and hearing
the ball. A paragraph about Messages, chat refresh, stale input and background
synchronisation does not teach those mechanics. Move shared application
behaviour to the appropriate guide instead of repeating it in each game.
Keep any essential player-facing limitation brief and separate from the
explanation of normal play; do not include a technical account of its cause.

Do not discard an important condition merely because its present wording is
technical. Rewrite the condition in terms of what the player can do. Nor
should editing remove required credits: for example, the original authors
and adaptation history of Axel Pong still need to be acknowledged.

## All language versions

For a newly commissioned language edition, write the explanation as a native
rulebook from the verified facts and agreed scope. Do not translate each source
sentence in order and then polish it. Choose the target language's ordinary
headings, connections and rhythm; the source book is a factual reference, not
a sentence pattern. This is not permission to invent rules, omit requirements
or rewrite a creator-supplied text protected above. In a faithful translation
of such a text, avoid literal syntax while preserving what the author chose
to say and the way they address the reader.

Translate the explanation, not the source sentence structure. Every version
must preserve the same rules, values, conditions and distinctions, but may
use different sentence boundaries and phrasing. Keep section structure and
cross-references coherent. Use the established names from the local interface
so the player can find the option being described.

In Polish, check inflection, agreement and repeated verbal nouns. Keep a
consistent way of addressing the player without starting every sentence
with “musisz” or “możesz”. In English, prefer direct verbs and natural use of
“you” rather than repeatedly calling the reader “the participant”. Czech,
Spanish, Russian and future translations need the same editorial attention
to their own syntax and conventions, not Polish or English constructions
with substituted words. Preserve meaningful distinctions in tense and aspect
and use the project's agreed terminology and variety of the language.

Read each complete changed language version. Back-translation can help check
meaning, but cannot establish natural phrasing. If a language cannot be
reliably assessed, identify the need for editorial review rather than calling
an unchecked translation finished. Do not claim that an automated detector
or a list of forbidden words proves that the writing is natural.

## Review and integration

Before preparing many rulebooks, check a representative explanation that
teaches ordinary play and a meaningful complication. When a user is
reviewing samples, apply their feedback to the writing method, not just the
quoted sentence. Do not claim that a revised checklist by itself proves the
rest of the rulebooks have improved. Review headings as well as paragraphs.

After checking the facts, set the implementation aside and read the changed
rulebook from beginning to end as a player. Perform this language review
separately from the mechanical checks. For a larger rewrite, assess how the
chapters fit together, not only the paragraphs that were individually edited.

Read the main explanation with the shortcut reference and the code set
aside. Walk through an ordinary turn or rally and one common difficulty
using only that explanation. At each decision, can the reader tell what
they are trying to do, what is allowed, how to do it and what follows? Do
not supply a missing step from your own knowledge of the game. Mark it as
a gap in the text. A list of correctly mentioned facts is not enough.

Check this for each substantial mechanic: drawing, discarding, bidding,
placing pieces or opening a setting that the text recommends. If the reader
must ask “but what do I press?”, the explanation is incomplete. If they can
find the key but cannot tell why or when to use it, the rule is incomplete.

Then review every paragraph for relevance. Keep needed rules and practical
instructions, but cut obvious statements, implementation commentary and
minor interface details that do not help the player. Look for concepts used
before they are explained, long chains of qualifications, chopped-up prose,
repeated facts and paragraphs that belong in another document. Also flag
short phrases whose meaning requires mental unpacking: missing actions,
unclear subjects and several card-effect pairs squeezed into one sentence.
Reading through speech synthesis can reveal awkward pauses and overloaded
sentences; report an actual listening check separately from a text review.

Where practical and authorised, ask someone unfamiliar with the game to
play from the rules without coaching them. Notice where they hesitate or
infer a wrong rule, not just whether they say the text is pleasant. Revise
that passage. An author's walkthrough is useful preparation, but is not an
independent beginner test; neither is an automated gameplay test. Be clear
about which review was actually done.

Compare the revision with the verified rules once more. Natural wording
must not silently change an obligation, a default, a scoring calculation or
an exception. Read the whole changed text in every affected language; a
sample does not stand in for this review. Passing format tests proves
neither readability nor completeness.

Use the existing [translation workflow](TRANSLATIONS.md). Complete books for
all supported languages (EN, PL, CS, ES and RU) live in
`tools/data/rulebooks/<LANG>/<game>.json` with their own
`option_chapters` mapping. Edit each document in its one authoritative source,
never in generated Ruby or a duplicate translation. Generate Ruby rulebooks
with `tools/compile-rulebooks.rb`. PO still owns interface messages, release
notes and the older root-level fallback books, not copies of these complete
editions; update and compile catalogs only when their messages also change.
When changing section IDs, update the relevant option-to-chapter
mapping and dependent links.

Preserve the native heading, paragraph and list structure of the rulebook
viewer. Use the supported bullet format for enumerations; do not insert
other raw Markdown that the viewer will not render. For Markdown guides,
use a clear heading hierarchy and working links, with blank lines around
headings and lists. Check that list items remain individually navigable
and that headings and contents links still lead to the correct text.

Keep checks proportionate to the actual changes. Validate changed sources,
translations, generated output and affected presentation paths. Check a new
layout or readout in the application when authorised, and state what was
actually checked. Do not treat a wording-only revision as a reason to change
game mechanics, rewrite other rulebooks or build a new release.

## Professional guidance and its use here

These sources support the teaching approach, not the rules of any particular
Power Games implementation. The wording examples above are our own project
examples, not quotations from these publications.

- [Paul Grogan in Producing Fun](https://naylorgames.com/blogs/blog/producing-fun-4-paul-grogan-rulebook-editor-content-creator),
  especially 59:26, 1:20:14 and 1:27:46: introduce the overall structure before
  detailed actions, do not write shorthand for people who already know the
  game, and use examples without hiding new rules inside them. This is the
  distinction behind our connected teaching passages and separate reference.
- [Resonym, Writing Rulebooks](https://resonym.com/writing-rulebooks/): learning
  a game and looking up a known rule are different reading tasks. Its
  discussion of uncoached rulebook tests informs our beginner walkthrough
  and the distinction between self-review and an actual new player's test.
- [Gary Chavez, Top Six Rules for Rulebook Writing](https://www.meeplemountain.com/top-six/top-six-rules-for-writing-rulebooks/):
  build later sections on information already introduced, explain a typical
  turn, revise in stages and keep names consistent instead of varying them
  for style alone.

Adapt such advice to an accessible computer game. Do not import physical
component inventories, layout tricks, mandatory artwork or thematic stories
just because a board-game guide recommends them. In Power Games, a useful
text explanation and the actual controls must stand on their own. External
advice does not override the user's scope, the implemented rules or the
project's language and accessibility requirements.
